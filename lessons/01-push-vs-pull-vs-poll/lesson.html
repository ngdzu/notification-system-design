Why Notification Systems Are Hard at Scale (Push vs. Pull vs. Poll)

Why this matters

Sending one notification is trivial — call an API, show a banner, done. The hard part shows up the moment you have hundreds of millions of users: one popular post can trigger millions of notifications in seconds, and every one has to arrive fast without knocking the system over. Before designing anything, we need a shared vocabulary for what a notification even is and the basic strategies for delivering it.

What is a notification, really?

A notification is a small, timely message telling a user something happened — “Alice liked your photo,” “Your order shipped.” It's small (a few lines of text plus an ID or link) and timely (it loses most of its value if it arrives an hour late instead of seconds late). That combination — tiny payload, strict freshness — is why notification systems are their own design problem.

Strategy ① — push: the server calls you

Push means the server proactively sends data the instant it's ready, without the client asking. Like a phone ringing: the caller initiates, you just react. It requires the server to hold an always-open channel to every connected client — somewhere to push to. Watch it below: messages stream the moment they exist.

Push: near-zero delay, but the server pays for every open connection, 24/7, whether or not anything happens.

Strategy ② — pull: you ask the server

Pull means the client asks “anything new for me?” on its own initiative — like checking your mailbox: nobody rings your doorbell, you walk out and look. Pulling on a fixed schedule (“check every 30 seconds”) is called polling. It's simpler and more robust — if the network drops, the client just asks again later — but notifications sit waiting on the server until the next ask.

Pull: the hollow dot is the client's question; the solid dot is the server's answer. In between — silence, even if news is waiting.

Strategy ③ — hybrid: what real systems run

Almost every real notification system is a hybrid: push as the fast path, pull as the safety net. The client gets pushes while its connection is alive; when the connection drops (subway, dead battery, flaky Wi-Fi) or the app reopens after hours away, it pulls once to catch up on anything it missed. Lesson 8 covers the push machinery in detail, and Lesson 10 makes pull a first-class fallback rather than an afterthought.

The core tension: latency vs. throughput

Two words will follow you through all 17 lessons. Latency is how long delivery takes — event happens → user sees it. Throughput is how many messages the whole system can process per second — what decides whether you survive a celebrity posting to 5 million followers. They pull in opposite directions: pushing everything instantly minimizes latency but maximizes constant load; batching, queuing, and polling raise throughput capacity at the cost of delay. Recap

  • Notification = small, timely message — freshness is part of its value.
  • Push: server-initiated, lowest latency, pays an always-on cost per connected client.
  • Pull / polling: client-initiated, resilient, latency bounded by the poll interval — and mostly wasted requests.
  • Real systems are hybrids — push first, pull as the safety net.
  • Latency vs. throughput is the single trade-off every later lesson revisits.
